Abstract

Social participation is widely thought to protect mental health in later life, yet much of the evidence comes from cross-sectional data, and less is known about whether the benefits differ by gender or by the type of activity. In this study we used four waves of a fictional panel study of adults aged 65 and older (N = 2,431) to examine the association between social participation and depressive symptoms over six years. Social participation was measured as the number of activities in which respondents took part at least monthly, including volunteering, religious services, clubs, and visits with friends. Depressive symptoms were measured with an eight-item scale. We estimated linear mixed models with random intercepts and adjusted for age, gender, education, wealth, marital status, chronic conditions, and functional limitations. Each additional activity was associated with fewer depressive symptoms at baseline and with a slower increase in symptoms over time. The association was stronger for volunteering and visits with friends than for religious attendance, and it was somewhat stronger among men than among women, although the gender difference was modest. Results were robust to the exclusion of respondents with probable depression at baseline and to alternative codings of participation. These findings suggest that sustaining opportunities for social participation, particularly volunteering and informal social contact, may help slow the rise of depressive symptoms in later life, and that programs should consider the specific activities that older men and women prefer. Future research should examine the mechanisms that link participation to mental health, including social support, sense of purpose, and physical activity.

Abstract

Objectives: Social participation may protect mental health in later life, but much of the evidence is cross-sectional. We examined whether social participation is associated with the level and trajectory of depressive symptoms among older adults.

Methods: We used four waves (2014–2020) of a fictional panel study of community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older (N = 2,431; 1,399 women; mean age 74.2 years for men and 75.3 years for women). Social participation was the number of six activities done at least monthly. Depressive symptoms were measured with an eight-item scale (range 0–8). Linear mixed models with random intercepts adjusted for age, gender, education, wealth, marital status, chronic conditions, and functional limitations.

Results: Each additional activity was associated with fewer depressive symptoms after adjustment (b = −0.21, SE = 0.03, p < .001). Symptoms rose over time (b = 0.10 per wave, SE = 0.02), but more slowly among respondents who took part in more activities (Activities × Time: b = −0.02, SE = 0.01, p < .05).

Discussion: Older adults who were more socially engaged reported fewer depressive symptoms and a slower rise in symptoms over 6 years. Because the data are observational, these associations should not be read as causal effects. Programs that support volunteering and informal social contact may benefit mental health in later life.
